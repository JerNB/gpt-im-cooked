Hypothesis testing asks: **Are the observed data sufficiently surprising under a proposed explanation to count as evidence against it?**

For example, suppose you suspect a coin favors heads:

- **Null hypothesis (\(H_0\)):** The probability of heads is 50.0% on each flip.
- **Alternative hypothesis (\(H_1\)):** The probability of heads is greater than 50.0% on each flip.

You flip the coin and count the heads. A **p-value** measures how likely a result at least as favorable to the alternative would be **assuming the null hypothesis is true**.

A small p-value gives evidence against the null. Compare it with a chosen significance level, such as 5.0%:

- If the p-value is at or below that level, **reject the null**.
- Otherwise, **fail to reject the null**; this does not prove it true.

The conditional-probability connection matters: **a p-value is calculated assuming the null is true. It is not the probability that the null is true given your data.**
