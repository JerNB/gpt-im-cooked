A two-way table organizes counts by two categories at once. Here’s your previous shopper example arranged as a table:

| Shopping method | Returned an item | Did not return | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Online | 9 | 11 | 20 |
| In-store | 6 | 19 | 25 |
| **Total** | **15** | **30** | **45** |

The key decision is familiar: **which group does the question ask about?**

“Among online shoppers, what percentage returned an item?” restricts us to the online **row**. Divide the cell by its row total: \(9/20\), giving **45.0% of online shoppers**.

“Among shoppers who returned an item, what percentage ordered online?” restricts us to the returned-item **column**. Divide the same cell by its column total: \(9/15\), giving **60.0% of shoppers who returned an item**.

The count stays nine; the reference group changes. This is exactly how your notes connect row and column percentages to conditional probability. ([Notes §2.1–2.2](materials/notes.md))

There’s also a useful consistency check: row percentages within one row add to 100%; column percentages within one column add to 100%. Each divides up its own reference group. ([Notes §2.3](materials/notes.md))
