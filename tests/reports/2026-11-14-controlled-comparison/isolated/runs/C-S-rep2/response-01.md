**Conditional probability means finding a share within a specified group.** Your notes read \(P(A\mid B)\) as “the probability of A among B.” The group after the bar—\(B\)—tells you who belongs in the denominator. [Notes §1.1–1.2](materials/notes.md)

\[
P(A\mid B)=\frac{\text{number in both A and B}}{\text{number in B}}
\]

Here’s a new example applying that rule. Suppose a class has 50 students:

- 20 are commuters.
- 8 of those commuters ride a bike.
- 10 students in the whole class ride a bike.

To find the probability that a student bikes **given that they commute**, restrict attention to the 20 commuters. Of those, 8 bike:

\[
P(\text{bikes}\mid\text{commutes})=\frac{8}{20}
\]

Report your answer as **40.0% of commuters ride a bike**. The denominator is 20 because the question has already restricted us to commuters.

Now reverse the question: among students who bike, what share commute? There are 10 students who bike, and 8 commute, giving **80.0% of students who bike are commuters**. The overlap is the same 8 students, but the reference group changes. That’s why reversing the order usually changes the answer. [Notes §1.3](materials/notes.md)

Your takeaway: **first name the reference group, then count the target cases inside it.** These counting rules assume equally weighted cases and a nonempty reference group.
