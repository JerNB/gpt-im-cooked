**Conditional probability means finding a share within a specified group.** Your notes read \(P(A\mid B)\) as “the probability of A among B.” Once you’re told \(B\), focus on the cases in \(B\). ([Notes §1.1–1.2](materials/notes.md))

The rule is:

\[
P(A\mid B)=
\frac{\text{number of cases in both A and B}}
{\text{number of cases in B}}
\]

**The group after the bar supplies the denominator.** Your notes assume equally weighted cases and a nonempty reference group.

For example, suppose a class has 50 students, including 20 commuters. Of those commuters, 8 cycle to campus.

“What is the probability a student cycles, **given that they commute**?”

You’re restricted to the **20 commuters**, and 8 of them cycle:

\[
P(\text{cycles}\mid\text{commutes})
=\frac{8}{20}\times100\%
=\boxed{40.0\%\text{ of commuters}}
\]

The denominator is 20 because the question asks about commuters.

**Reversing the condition changes the question.** Suppose there are 10 cyclists altogether. Asking what share of cyclists commute gives:

\[
P(\text{commutes}\mid\text{cycles})
=\frac{8}{10}\times100\%
=\boxed{80.0\%\text{ of cyclists}}
\]

The same 8 students appear in both numerators, but the reference groups differ. That’s why order matters. ([Notes §1.3](materials/notes.md))
