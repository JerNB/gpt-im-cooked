Conditional probability means **restricting attention to a particular group, then finding the share within that group.**

Your notes read \(P(A\mid B)\) as “the probability of A **among B**.” The group after the bar—\(B\)—is your **reference group**. [Notes §1.1–1.2](materials/notes.md)

For equally weighted cases and a nonempty reference group:

\[
P(A\mid B)=\frac{\text{number in both A and B}}{\text{number in B}}.
\]

Imagine a class of 30 students:

- 20 are commuters.
- 10 ride bikes.
- 8 are both commuters and bike riders.

“What is the probability that a student rides a bike, **given that they’re a commuter**?”

The condition tells us to consider only the **20 commuters**. Among those, 8 ride bikes. Dividing 8 by 20 gives:

**40.0% of commuters ride bikes.**

The denominator is 20 because the question restricts us to commuters. Using all 30 students would answer a different question. [Notes §1.2](materials/notes.md)

Now reverse the question: “What is the probability that a student is a commuter, **given that they ride a bike**?”

We now consider the **10 bike riders**. Of those, 8 are commuters:

**80.0% of bike riders are commuters.**

The same 8 students appear in both calculations, but the reference group changes. That’s why \(P(A\mid B)\) and \(P(B\mid A)\) usually differ. [Notes §1.3](materials/notes.md)
