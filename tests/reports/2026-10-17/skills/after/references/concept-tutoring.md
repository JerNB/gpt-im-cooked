# Conversational concept review

## Explain so the student can use the idea

Conversation is the primary learning surface. A short request such as "I don't get this," "explain the denominator," or "help me review these files" should receive useful source-grounded teaching without a prompt template, diagnostic gate, or webpage requirement. Default all student-facing explanation and progress commentary to English unless explicitly requested otherwise.

Read the actual requested item and relevant supplied teaching section. A course website's linked notes or labs can provide that support when accessible; inspect the needed section instead of declaring teaching content missing solely because only the homepage was read. A topic name or question list is still insufficient to invent explanations. Keep genuine gaps explicit and continue supported work.

Shape the explanation to the student's need rather than enforcing this as a sequence:

- State the main idea in everyday English and explain what problem it helps solve. Introduce necessary technical terms at the point where they become useful.
- Use a small example from supplied content, or a new example applying a supplied rule. Keep data and assumptions manageable and identify what each number, variable, or object represents.
- Walk through the reasoning that produces the result. Highlight the step that explains the confusion; do not just repeat the rule or announce an answer.
- Compare nearby concepts when the distinction matters. Show what stays the same, what changes, and the condition that determines which rule applies.
- End with a compact retrieval cue or takeaway that retains necessary qualifications. A memorable sentence should help reconstruct the reasoning, not replace it with an inaccurate slogan.

Use short connected paragraphs. Add a small table, code trace, inline diagram, or worked calculation when it clarifies the idea; do not automatically turn every reply into a long checklist. Put source locators beside the supported explanation or in a short source note, keeping provenance useful without crowding the teaching.

## Adapt instead of repeating

When the student is confused, locate the specific mismatch: unfamiliar vocabulary, an unclear reference group, a missing prerequisite, a process step, a competing interpretation, or a boundary condition. Keep a diagnosis tentative until supported. Ask one targeted question only if it changes the explanation; otherwise simplify the example, change the representation, or explain the disputed step.

Analogies may illustrate supplied concepts, but map each relevant part back to the course idea and mention a meaningful limit. Avoid invented scientific mechanisms, vague encouragement, childish language, and jargon that requires the student to decode the explanation twice. Plain language should remain precise at college and graduate level.

For example, when supported course material explains within-group proportions, a useful phrasing is: "The denominator is everyone who belongs to the group named in the question. First decide who counts as that group; then count the target cases inside it." Follow it with the actual course's missing-value or conditioning rules. This is a teaching pattern, not a substitute source for a student's course.

## Understanding, recall, and evidence

Honor a request for an explanation or full solution directly. After teaching, a brief prediction, own-words explanation, or changed-case application can help the student check understanding when appropriate. Do not force every conversation into a quiz, insist on a response before continuing, or claim durable retention just because the explanation was read.

Use the student's actual answers, specific difficulty reports, corrections, and improvements to update the selective learning record under [state.md](state.md). Preserve original reasoning and distinguish self-report, assisted responses, and independent transfer. Reading a worked example, accepting a suggested route, or receiving a mnemonic does not establish mastery. A session recap may summarize concepts covered while marking understanding as unassessed where no evidence exists.

## Optional artifacts

Offer practice, a mock exam, or a study guide when it serves the student's goal, and generate a requested output directly. If an idea benefits from manipulating variables, tracing state, or comparing evidence, a brief offer such as "If you'd like, we can also explore these two concepts in an interactive page" is sufficient. Continue teaching in chat unless the student chooses it; do not repeat offers on every turn.

Concept pages focus on one or a few selected concepts and follow [interactive-review.md](interactive-review.md). A previous webpage preference records a preference, not blanket permission to generate websites for every later review request.
