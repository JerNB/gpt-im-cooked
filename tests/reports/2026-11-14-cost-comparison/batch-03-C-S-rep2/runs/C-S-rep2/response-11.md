Your saved next step is **two-way tables**. You previously demonstrated conditional probability, with a later retention check still due. We’ll build on that reference-group reasoning.

A two-way table counts cases by two categories at once. Here’s a new example applying your notes §2:

| Students | Cycle | Don’t cycle | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Commuters | 12 | 18 | 30 |
| Residents | 8 | 12 | 20 |
| Total | 20 | 30 | 50 |

The same cell—12 commuters who cycle—can answer two different questions:

- **Among commuters, what percentage cycle?** Use the commuter row total: \(12/30 \times 100 = 40.0\%\) **of commuters**.
- **Among cyclists, what percentage commute?** Use the cyclist column total: \(12/20 \times 100 = 60.0\%\) **of cyclists**.

The numerator stays 12 because both questions concern students who commute **and** cycle. The denominator changes because each question names a different reference group. This is exactly the conditional-probability rule you practiced earlier. ([Notes §1.2 and §2.1–2.2](materials/notes.md))

There’s also a useful check: row percentages within one row add to 100%; column percentages within one column add to 100%. For commuters, that’s 40.0% who cycle plus 60.0% who don’t. ([Notes §2.3](materials/notes.md))
