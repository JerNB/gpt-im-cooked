Conditional probability asks: **within a particular group, what share also meets another condition?** Your notes read \(P(A\mid B)\) as “the probability of A among B.” The group after the bar—\(B\)—is your **reference group**. ([Notes §1.1–1.2](materials/notes.md))

For equally weighted cases and a nonempty reference group:

\[
P(A\mid B)=
\frac{\text{number of cases in both A and B}}
{\text{number of cases in B}}.
\]

For a new example using that rule, imagine 100 students:

- 40 are commuters.
- 16 of those commuters bike to campus.
- 20 students in total bike to campus.

What is the probability that a student bikes **given that they commute**?

“Given that they commute” restricts us to the **40 commuters**. Of those, 16 bike:

\[
P(\text{bikes}\mid\text{commutes})
=\frac{16}{40}
=\boxed{\text{40.0\% of commuters}}.
\]

The denominator is 40 because we’re asking about commuters. The other students fall outside the reference group.

**Reversing the condition changes the question.** If we ask what share commute **among students who bike**, the reference group becomes the 20 students who bike:

\[
P(\text{commutes}\mid\text{bikes})
=\frac{16}{20}
=\boxed{\text{80.0\% of students who bike}}.
\]

The overlap stays at 16, but the denominator changes. That is why \(P(A\mid B)\) and \(P(B\mid A)\) usually differ. ([Notes §1.3](materials/notes.md))
